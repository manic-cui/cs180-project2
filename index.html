Part 1.1: Convolutions from scratch

Convolution slides a flipped kernel over the image and sums the products. I pad with zeros so the output keeps the input size ("same"). For kernel height kh I put kh//2 zero rows on top and kh-1-kh//2 at the bottom, which is how scipy centers the output, also for even kernels like Dx.

Four loops:

def conv_4loops(im, k):
    H,W=im.shape
    kh,kw=k.shape
    ph=kh//2
    pw=kw//2
    kf=k[::-1,::-1]      # flip kernel, this is convolution not correlation
    # pad zero, output same size
    padded=np.zeros((H+kh-1, W+kw-1))
    padded[ph:ph+H, pw:pw+W]=im
    out=np.zeros((H,W))
    for i in range(H):
        for j in range(W):
            s=0.0
            for u in range(kh):
                for v in range(kw):
                    s=s+padded[i+u, j+v]*kf[u,v]
            out[i,j]=s
    return out

Two loops, the inner two become one numpy multiply and sum:

def conv_2loops(im, k):
    H,W=im.shape
    kh,kw=k.shape
    ph=kh//2
    pw=kw//2
    kf=k[::-1,::-1]
    padded=np.zeros((H+kh-1, W+kw-1))
    padded[ph:ph+H, pw:pw+W]=im
    out=np.zeros((H,W))
    for i in range(H):
        for j in range(W):
            # inner two loop change to numpy
            out[i,j]=np.sum(padded[i:i+kh, j:j+kw]*kf)
    return out

Runtime. The results are identical up to floating point. Pure Python loops are about 200x slower than scipy for the 9x9 box. The two loop version still makes one numpy call per pixel (128,000 calls), so for the tiny Dx kernel it is even slower than four plain loops. scipy is C code and can switch to FFT for big kernels.

Boundaries. My code and scipy with boundary='fill' treat everything outside the image as black. For the box filter this gives a dark frame around the picture (the average near the border includes zeros), and for the derivative filters a fake edge along the border. scipy can also mirror the image (boundary='symm'), which avoids both; I use that in Part 1.2 and 1.3.

Part 1.2: Finite difference operator

Convolve the cameraman (float in [0, 1]) with Dx and Dy to get the partial derivatives, then the gradient magnitude per pixel:

|∇I| = sqrt( (∂I/∂x)² + (∂I/∂y)² )

The edge image keeps the pixels where the magnitude is above a threshold.

I chose 0.25. Below 0.2 the grass becomes a field of white dots, since the 2-pixel filter reacts to every small jump in the texture. At 0.3 the noise is gone but the buildings on the right and the far tripod leg start to vanish. 0.25 keeps the coat, camera, all tripod legs and most of the skyline with only a few grass speckles. Any threshold that removes all grass dots also removes the weak but real building edges, so it is a trade-off.

Part 1.3: Derivative of Gaussian filter

The difference filter only looks at two pixels, so it is noisy. I build a 2D Gaussian from cv2.getGaussianKernel(11, 1.5) and the outer product g @ g.T, blur the image first, then repeat 1.2.

Differences: the gradient values are much smaller (a step is spread over several pixels), so the threshold drops from 0.25 to 0.06. The edges are thicker and continuous instead of broken dashes, and the grass texture is mostly gone because the blur removes it before the derivative. Less noise and fewer missing edges at the same time.

DoG: one convolution instead of two

Convolution is associative, (I * G) * Dx = I * (G * Dx), so I convolve the Gaussian with Dx and Dy once to get two DoG filters and then convolve the image only once.

Verification: the gradient magnitudes from the two ways differ by at most 1.1e-15 and the edge maps are identical for every threshold.

Part 2.1: Image sharpening (unsharp masking)

A Gaussian blur keeps the low frequencies; original minus blur is the high frequencies (edges, texture). Adding α times the high frequencies back makes the image look sharper:

sharp = I + α (I − G*I) = ((1+α) δ − α G) * I

The right side is a single convolution with the unsharp mask kernel. Both versions give the same image (max difference 6e-14).

Taj Mahal, σ = 1.5

α = 0.5 to 1 makes the carvings and the people crisper. At α = 2 halos appear around the dark trees. At α = 4 the sky gets grainy because JPEG noise is high frequency too, and the dome looks outlined. Sharpening adds no information, it only boosts existing edges, so the good α depends on how noisy the picture is.

Doe Library, σ = 2

The columns and window frames pop out at α = 1, α = 2 starts to look harsh. The flat gray sky stays flat, it has no high frequencies to boost.

Evaluation: blur a sharp image, then sharpen it

A sharp lion photo, blurred with σ = 2, then unsharp masked with the same σ.

Sharpening makes the blurred lion look crisper again, but the fine hairs of the mane do not come back: they were averaged away by the blur and are not in the image anymore. At α = 5 the edges that survived get halos while the fur stays smooth. Unsharp masking boosts the high frequencies that are still there, it is not the inverse of blurring.

Part 2.2: Hybrid images

Hybrid = low frequencies of one picture + high frequencies of another. Close up the high frequencies win, from far away only the low frequencies are left. Low pass: Gaussian with σlow. High pass: image minus Gaussian with σhigh. All hybrids are grayscale.

Alignment uses the starter code with the two eye positions typed in instead of clicked: recenter on the eye midpoint, rescale so the eye distances match, rotate one image, crop both to the same size. I pass the low frequency picture as the one that gets rotated, since black corners matter less on a blurred image. The black borders come from this padding and rotation.

Cutoff. A Gaussian with std σ pixels drops a frequency f (cycles/pixel) to half at f ≈ 0.19/σ. So σlow = 10 keeps details coarser than about 50 pixels per cycle and σhigh = 4 keeps details finer than about 20 pixels, on the 498x827 aligned image.

σlow = 5 leaves the cat sharp enough to fight with Derek up close (double exposure); σlow = 20 turns the cat into a blob that never reads as a cat. σhigh = 2 leaves only thin outlines of Derek, σhigh = 8 keeps so much of him that he is visible at every distance. σhigh = 4, σlow = 10 gives the cleanest switch; σlow should be clearly larger than σhigh so the two images do not share a band.

House cat and lion

Cat up close, lion from far, aligned on the eyes. σhigh = 4, σlow = 6. The lion needs a smaller σlow than Nutmeg because its face is small in the frame; with a bigger blur the mane and the grass merge into one blob.

Failure: Doe Library, close and far

Does not work. Both pictures are only straight lines and repeated windows, so the blurred building is just smeared lines that never form an object of its own; at every distance you see the sharp building plus smearing. The perspective is also different, so the lines cannot be aligned by scale and rotation. Hybrids need two images with similar coarse structure, and the low frequency one needs big smooth regions that survive the blur.

Part 2.3: Gaussian and Laplacian stacks

A stack is a pyramid without downsampling, all levels keep the full size. Gaussian stack: level 0 is the image, each next level is the previous one blurred with σ doubling (2, 4, 8, 16, 32), so detail disappears at the same rate as in a pyramid. Laplacian stack: difference of neighbouring Gaussian levels, the last level is the blurriest Gaussian itself, so the sum of all Laplacian levels is the original (reconstruction error 1e-16). Implemented from scratch with the Gaussian kernel from cv2.getGaussianKernel.

Part 2.4: Multiresolution blending

With Laplacian stacks LA, LB and a Gaussian stack GM of the mask (1 where A shows), each level is blended separately and the levels are added:

Lout,i = GM,i · LA,i + (1 − GM,i) · LB,i , out = Σi Lout,i

The mask gets blurrier at each level, so fine detail is cut with a sharp seam (a sharp cut between two textures looks natural) while colour and shading are mixed over a wide band, which hides the seam. 6 levels, σ = 2 doubling, colour images channel by channel.

The most important thing I learned

Blur and detail are the same thing from two sides. A Gaussian keeps the low frequencies, original minus blur is the high frequencies, and with that one split you can sharpen (add the detail back), make a hybrid (detail of one picture on the blur of another) or blend (split into bands and pick a seam width per band). The practical lesson: most of the time went into thresholds, sigmas and alignment, not into the filters, and the right numbers depend on the image size and content.
